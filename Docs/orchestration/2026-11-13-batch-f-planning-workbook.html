Six findings (read in code)

#FindingWhy it matters
F1What scrolls is the page area, not the window. AppLayout.tsx is a full-height flex layout whose <main> has overflow-y-auto. The top bar (search, Import, Mira, family selector, theme) sits outside it and never scrolls.A sticky index must stick inside <main>, and scroll-spy must watch <main>, not the window. The router does not scroll to hashes by itself.
F2The tab bar is a rounded card with seven links on the left and a utility slot on the right. It is not sticky. Only the Overview uses the slot today, for a page-local “Search investments…” box.That slot is the one search (K), the section index and the PDF button (I) all want. And on a long page the tabs scroll out of view, so there is no way to switch tab without scrolling back up.
F3Section counts differ a lot: Overview 7 (8 with Batch D’s graph), Allocation 4, Overlap 4, Other Analytics 4 groups holding 6 cards, Performance 3, Holdings 2, Transactions 2. The fund page’s tabs have 5 to 6 sections each (section 2).The long tabs need the index; the 2-section tabs barely need one. A rule (“show when 3 or more”) avoids clutter.
F4There is no stable id on most sections. SectionCard makes its heading id with useId() (a random value), and some sections are not cards at all (the Overview’s summary strip, the holdings table, the Cash Flow strip). A few headings have fixed ids (holdings-title, sip-title).Every section needs a stable anchor first; that is the real groundwork of this batch.
F5The sidebar is 224 px wide and the page area has 24 px padding, so at 1280 px wide the content is about 1008 px. My estimate of the seven tab labels at the current size is about 620 px, leaving about 370 px.Not enough to expand even a 4-section tab inline (about 430 px by estimate), let alone the Overview’s 7 sections (about 730 px). Section 3 shows this.
F7The fund page is a separate frame (FundPageFrame.tsx): a breadcrumb, a fund header, a stat band above the tabs (Overview only), then three underline-style tabs (Overview, Overlap, Insights). The tab strip is a plain row of links and is not sticky.With only three short tabs there is plenty of room, so your expanding-tab idea fits here (section 3). But the two frames look different, and one component should serve both (Q2 B).
F6Search’s plan and the PDF export (Batch I) both want a slot in the same header area, and Batch E adds a “data dates” strip under the tabs.The layout has to be planned as one stack: header, tabs, section index, data strip, content (section 5).

3. How the index looks

Your idea, plus four alternatives, drawn on the Allocation and Overview tabs at a 1280-pixel laptop.

Does your idea fit? (an estimate)

Your idea: the active tab opens in place to show its sections, so the tab row reads Overview › Summary · Value over time · SIPs · Mutual Funds · Sold funds · AMC Allocation · Family Members · Quick Insights, with the other six tabs still on the row. These are my estimates, not measurements, from the label lengths and the current tab size.

Estimated widths in pixels. The row has about 1008 px of room at 1280 px wide (the page area), less the card’s own padding and the search slot. Green fits, amber does not, grey is the room left.

On the fund page your idea fits: it has only three tabs (about 214 px by estimate), so even six sections fit on the row. On the Mutual Funds tabs it does not: your idea fits only on tabs with three sections or fewer (Performance; Holdings and Transactions would not show an index anyway). It does not fit on Allocation, Overlap, Other Analytics or the Overview, which are the tabs that most need it, unless the other tabs fold away while the active one is open (option A below). That is why I propose a variation, and one component that looks the same on both frames.

The options

Option

Made-up numbers; laptop at 1280 px. In A, the other tabs fold into a “More” menu while sections are open, otherwise the row overflows. B and C are drawn on the Allocation tab; the last button draws B on the fund page, under its underline tabs.

OptionWhat it isStrengthWeakness
A. Your idea: the active tab expandsThe active tab opens inline into its sections; the others shrink or fold into “More”.Closest to what you described; no extra row; the tab and its sections read as one thing.Does not fit on the four longest tabs at laptop width by my estimate, so the other tabs must fold away; the tab row changes shape on every click; folding tabs into “More” hides navigation exactly when a user is exploring.
B. A section row under the tabs (my pick)A slim second row directly under the tab bar, visually attached to the active tab, listing its sections as small chips. The active section is highlighted as you scroll; a click jumps. Both rows stay stuck at the top while scrolling.Always fits (it can scroll sideways if it ever overflows); the tabs never change; the same behaviour on every tab; keeps tabs visible while scrolling, which also fixes F2.Uses two stuck rows of height (about 90 px by estimate), which matters on a short window. Mitigation in section 4.
C. A side railA thin “On this page” list down the right side of the content, sticking as you scroll.No height cost; shows every section at once; familiar from documentation sites.Takes about 170 px of width from the cards (already tight at 1008 px); hidden or crowded below about 1280 px; far from where the eye is when choosing a tab.
D. A “Jump to” menuA single drop-down button in the tab bar’s right slot listing the sections.Smallest; works at any width; no height cost.Hides the sections behind a click, so the page still feels like a black hole until you open it; no scroll-spy position.
E. Collapsible sectionsEach section becomes an accordion that can be folded, so a page can be shortened to its headings.Solves length directly; the user controls it.Hides data by default or by habit; a large redesign of every card; does not answer “where is X”.
My recommendation: B, on both frames. It keeps your idea (the tab and its sections read as one group, and clicking jumps) and avoids the width problem. If you want your original shape, the closest honest version of A is B with the sections row connected to the active tab by a small notch, which is how I would draw it. D is a cheap addition that can sit beside B later if you want it.

4. How it behaves

The details of option B (and most of them apply to any option). Defaults I will use unless you say otherwise.

BehaviourHow it works
Where it sticksThe tab row and the section row stay at the top of the page area while the content scrolls beneath them. The top bar (search, Import, family selector) is outside the scroll area and unaffected.
Height budgetAt a short window such as 1280×600, two sticky rows cost about 90 px (an estimate), leaving about 440 px for content. To protect short windows, the tab row hides while scrolling down and returns when scrolling up, leaving only the section row stuck (about 40 px) most of the time. Both stay visible at the top of the page.
JumpClicking a chip scrolls the page area so the section’s heading lands just below the sticky rows (smooth scroll, instant for people who prefer reduced motion). Focus moves to the section’s heading so keyboard and screen-reader users land there.
Scroll-spyThe chip of the section nearest the top is highlighted as you scroll. It watches the page area, not the window. A section that is still loading keeps its place (see Risks).
AddressThe jump writes the section into the page address (#allocation-market-cap), so a link or a refresh returns to that section, and the Back button steps back through jumps. Opening a tab with no hash starts at the top, as today.
Keyboard and screen readersEach chip is a link to the section; the row is a labelled navigation region; the current section carries aria-current. Tab, Enter and Escape behave as on any link.
On the fund pageThe fund header and the stat band scroll away as today; the tab strip (Overview, Overlap, Insights) sticks, with the section row directly under it. The chips take the same look as on the Mutual Funds frame, adapted to the underline tabs. Your answer to Q3 is read as covering the fund page’s tab strip too.
When the index showsOn tabs with 3 or more sections (a setting). On the others the row is simply not drawn, and the tab bar still sticks.
Loading, empty and errorA section that is empty or failed still appears in the index so the list never jumps around; an empty tab (no funds yet) shows no index.
With the other batchesOrder of the stack, top to bottom: page title, tab row, section row, the data strip from Batch E, then the content. Search (K) and the PDF button (I) use the tab bar’s right slot (section 5).

Before and after

Before

  1. The user opens Allocation or the Overview and scrolls a long page with no sense of what is below.
  2. The tabs scroll away; to switch tab or jump to a lower card the user scrolls all the way up or down.
  3. There is no link to a section.

After (option B)

  1. A chip row names every section of the tab and highlights where you are.
  2. The tabs and the chips stay at the top; a click jumps to the section.
  3. Every section has a stable link that survives a refresh.

5. One list, shared

The index, search’s “page sections” results and the PDF export must all show the same sections. Define the list once.

PieceWhat it is
The listOne configuration per tab, for both frames (the seven Mutual Funds tabs and the fund page’s three): an ordered list of entries { id, title, anchor, tier }, kept next to the tab definitions in MutualFundsFrame.tsx (where TAB_DEFS already lives) and FundPageFrame.tsx, or in the route registry. tier lets Standard hide Pro sections later (Batch C4); it is unused today.
The anchorEach section element gets id from the list, plus a scroll-margin-top that equals the sticky height. SectionCard gains an anchor property; the sections that are not cards (the Overview’s strip and table, the Cash Flow strip) get a small wrapper.
Who reads itThe index (this batch); search’s “page sections” results (K); the PDF export’s section selection and order (I); Batch E’s strip entries can reuse the same ids. Nothing else may hard-code a section list.
A guard testA test checks that every id in the list exists on its page, and that every card on a page appears in the list or is marked as intentionally excluded.

The header stack

Layout

Laptop, made-up. The search box (K) and the PDF button (I) sit in the tab bar’s right slot; the data strip (E) sits under the section row. Nothing uses the same space.

9. Risks, tests and checks

RiskWhat we do
Two sticky rows eat too much of a short windowThe tab row hides while scrolling down and returns on scrolling up; both are checked at 1280×600; the section row is only about 40 px.
Sticky and scroll-spy are wrong because the page area, not the window, scrollsBoth are built against the page area from the start; tests simulate scrolling inside it.
Sections load late and shift the page, so the highlight or a jump lands in the wrong placeJumps re-measure after loading settles; scroll-spy uses the section’s own position, not a fixed height; Batch D’s lazy graph is a named test case.
The list and the page disagree (a section is added and the index misses it)The guard test fails when a card on a page is not in the list or marked excluded.
The smooth scroll or the sticky rows bother some usersReduced-motion users get an instant jump; the tab row’s hide-on-scroll can be turned off by a setting.
The two frames (seven tabs; three underline tabs) look or behave differentlyOne component with a frame-specific wrapper; both frames are in the same mockups and the same checks.
The index and the Overview’s page-local search fight for the right slotThey are placed in the stack in section 5; the slot rule is agreed with search’s plan (K) before building.
The Back button fills with jumpsA jump replaces the hash entry rather than adding one for repeated jumps within a tab (to be confirmed in the spec).
A rarely used tab (2 sections) looks inconsistent without the rowThe tab bar still sticks on every tab; only the chip row is skipped, by the 3-section rule.
