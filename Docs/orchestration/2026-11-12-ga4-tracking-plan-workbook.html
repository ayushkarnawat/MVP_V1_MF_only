In short: what GA4 does by itself, and what needs our own events

GA4 does on its own

  • Where visitors came from: source, medium, campaign, referrer, and links tagged with UTM
  • Device, browser, operating system, language, approximate country and city
  • New versus returning visitors, sessions, users, how long a page was in front of them
  • Page views on the website
  • Clicks on links that leave the site, and file downloads
  • Scrolling to the bottom of a page (90 percent)
  • Reports built from events: funnels, paths, retention, audiences, key events
  • Realtime view and DebugView for testing

Needs our own events

  • Clicks on specific buttons and links on the website
  • Which website sections were seen, time per section, sections skipped
  • Page views, if the site is a single-page site
  • Which call to action led to the app (the click on the sign-up or log-in link)
  • Labels such as a campaign or page type attached to a visit, if you want them

Neither can do

  • Heatmaps or replays of what people did on screen (a different tool)
  • Anything involving amounts, holdings or names (we never send them, and the website does not have them)
  • Follow a visitor into the web app. The app is outside GA4, so the trail stops at the link click
  • Count people who say no to cookies
Rule of thumb. GA4 tells you who came to the website, from where, and how each page did. Our own events tell you what they did on the page.

Limits that apply to everything

  • Consent undercount. With opt-in, people who decline are not counted (or only partly modelled, see question 5). Counts understate real visits.
  • Ad blockers block Google’s script for many users, more in finance and tech audiences.
  • Retention. Event-level data in custom Explorations is kept 2 months by default, up to 14 months if you change it. Standard reports are not affected. The change is not retroactive, so set it before collecting.
  • Limits per property (standard): about 50 event-scoped custom dimensions per third-party sources, which Google’s own page I could confirm only in part. The schema must stay small.
  • Data leaves India. Google processes it abroad, which is why counsel review matters (question 15).

6. What consent looks like (website)

You asked whether the consent we would ask for is about cookies. The answer first, then the screens.

What we would be asking permission for

  • Yes, cookies, and a little more. Google’s analytics tag stores small identifier cookies in the browser, which is how it tells one visitor from another, and it sends usage events to Google: which page, which button, which section. The permission covers both the cookies and the events.
  • Not everything. Cookies the website needs to work are strictly necessary and need no permission.
  • Never part of it: amounts, holdings, fund names, member names, PAN, folio, email, phone. That is the rule you chose in Q8. The website does not hold that data anyway.
  • One place to ask now: the website, where visitors are anonymous (a banner and a cookie settings link). The web app is parked (below).
The wording follows the common cookie-banner pattern: a short, plain question, Accept and Reject side by side, and a link to a Cookie Policy. The details (what is measured, that Google processes it outside India, what is never sent) move to that linked Cookie Policy page, and counsel must check that the short banner plus the policy says enough. “Reject” is drawn as prominent as “Accept” on purpose: a hidden or weaker refuse button is a known way to make consent invalid. Every word needs counsel’s review (Q15).
