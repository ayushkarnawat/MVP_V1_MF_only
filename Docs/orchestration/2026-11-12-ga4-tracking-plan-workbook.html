In short: what GA4 does by itself, and what needs our own events

GA4 does on its own

  • Where visitors came from: source, medium, campaign, referrer, and links tagged with UTM
  • Device, browser, operating system, language, approximate country and city
  • New versus returning visitors, sessions, users, how long a page was in front of them
  • Page views on the website
  • Clicks on links that leave the site, and file downloads
  • Scrolling to the bottom of a page (90 percent)
  • Reports built from events: funnels, paths, retention, audiences, key events
  • Realtime view and DebugView for testing

Needs our own events

  • Page views inside the web app (a single-page app, and we send clean page names)
  • Clicks on specific buttons and links
  • Which sections were seen, time per section, sections skipped
  • Sign-up and login steps, onboarding steps, import started, finished or failed
  • Choices inside the app: period selected, benchmark selected, tab switched, filters used
  • Search: what kind of result was clicked (labels only, never the text typed)
  • Labels such as plan tier attached to a visitor, if you want them
  • Carrying the website source into the app sign-up, if the two are not one stream

Neither can do

  • Heatmaps or replays of what people did on screen (a different tool, and not recommended for financial screens)
  • Anything involving amounts, holdings or names (we never send them)
  • Link Google’s data to a person in our database, unless a user ID is sent (Q7)
  • Count people who say no to cookies
Rule of thumb. GA4 tells you who came, from where, and how each page did. Our own events tell you what they did inside the product.

Limits that apply to everything

  • Consent undercount. With opt-in, people who decline are not counted (or only partly modelled, see question 4). Counts understate real use.
  • Ad blockers block Google’s script for many users, more in finance and tech audiences.
  • Retention. Event-level data in custom Explorations is kept 2 months by default, up to 14 months if you change it. Standard reports are not affected. The change is not retroactive, so set it before collecting.
  • Limits per property (standard): about 50 event-scoped and 25 user-scoped custom dimensions per third-party sources, which Google’s own page I could confirm only in part. The schema must stay small.
  • No per-customer deletion and no joining to our database unless a user ID is sent (question 7).
  • Data leaves India. Google processes it abroad, which is why counsel review matters (question 15).

6. What consent looks like

You asked whether the consent we would ask for is about cookies. The answer first, then the screens.

What we would be asking permission for

  • Yes, cookies, and a little more. Google’s analytics tag stores small identifier cookies in the browser, which is how it tells one visitor from another, and it sends usage events to Google: which page, which button, which section. The permission covers both the cookies and the events.
  • Not everything. The cookies that keep someone signed in are strictly necessary and need no permission. The consents the app already has (terms, CAS import) are separate and unchanged.
  • Never part of it: amounts, holdings, fund names, member names, PAN, folio, email, phone. That is the rule you chose in Q8.
  • Two places to ask: the website, where visitors are anonymous (a banner), and the app, where users are signed in (a one-time dialog and a switch in Profile).
The wording now follows the common cookie-banner pattern: a short, plain question, Accept and Reject side by side, and a link to a Cookie Policy. The details (what is measured, that Google processes it outside India, what is never sent) move to that linked Cookie Policy page, and counsel must check that the short banner plus the policy says enough. “Reject” is drawn as prominent as “Accept” on purpose: a hidden or weaker refuse button is a known way to make consent invalid. Every word needs counsel’s review (Q15).
