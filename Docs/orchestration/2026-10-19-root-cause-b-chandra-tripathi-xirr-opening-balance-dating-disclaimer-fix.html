1 · The distortion, and the real file that found it

Tripathi household · chandra.pdf

Every OPENING_BALANCE-type transaction is dated at the statement's own start date (date=lot.start, backend/app/services/import_/confirm_people.py) — not the real historical purchase date, because the CAS the household uploaded has no record of when those units were actually bought, only what they cost and what they're worth today. When a large share of a fund's cost basis carries this placeholder date, and the statement window is short relative to the investor's true history, lifetime_xirr / current_holdings_xirr collapse years of real compounding into an artificially short holding period and report an annualized rate nowhere near reality.

This fix is deliberately a disclaimer, not a corrected number. There is no real purchase date to recover from a CAS that only ever printed a cost and a closing balance — guessing one would just replace one wrong number with another, less honestly. Showing the real number alongside an honest "here's what it looks like excluding the part we can't date" is the only move that doesn't fabricate data.

2 · Why a disclaimer, not a guessed date

The alternative — inventing a plausible historical purchase date for opening-balance units — was considered and rejected. Any date picked (fund launch, NFO date, an average of the household's other transaction dates) would be fabricated, not derived from anything the CAS actually states, and would silently change lifetime_xirr for every household that has ever carried a legacy holding — nearly all of them. A disclaimer leaves the existing number exactly as it is (verified unchanged for every account, §8), and adds a second, honestly-computed number only when there's real evidence the first one is specifically distorted by this mechanism.

3 · The lineage-scoping correction, closed

Corrected before any code shipped, not after

Earlier in this same working session, before any code was written, "full per-unit lineage through switches" was discussed as the more rigorous design — tracing which specific units, including ones that have since passed through a SWITCH_OUT/SWITCH_IN pair, still carry the opening-balance's placeholder date versus a real one. That was overstated as a difficulty to solve, not a design question to settle.

What was found

There is no persisted field correlating a SWITCH_OUT row with its paired SWITCH_IN row anywhere in the schema — confirmed by reading backend/app/models/transaction.py in full. The only columns are folio_id, import_id, type, date, amount, units, nav, stamp_duty, origin, cost_source, and an internal occurrence dedup counter — nothing that links one row to another. The only place a switch's two legs are ever paired is a transient, in-memory partner: dict[int, NormalizedTransaction] inside backend/app/services/import_/opening_balance.py, built and discarded during one import run. It never reaches the database, so there is nothing left for calculate_dashboard_xirr() — which runs long after import, against persisted rows only — to trace through later.

The corrected design

Lineage tracing is scoped to same-scheme-only: a SWITCH_IN transaction always creates a fresh, non-opening-tagged lot, regardless of whether the money it carries originally came from an opening-balance lot in the source scheme. This isn't just a concession to what the schema supports — it's also the more defensible reading of the problem. A switch is a real, precisely-dated transaction: the investor (or their distributor) explicitly chose, on a known date, to move money between schemes. That date genuinely re-anchors how long the money has been in the new scheme; it isn't a fabricated placeholder the way the opening balance's date is.

Both sides of this boundary are proven by one test: test_opening_lineage_switch_out_exits_lineage_switch_in_does_not_inherit_it (§7) — the SWITCH_OUT side correctly exits lineage, and the new SWITCH_IN lot in the destination scheme is correctly not tagged opening, even though its money traces back to an opening-balance lot.

4 · Decision log — every open question resolved

1Two-condition trigger, not a single threshold — but, as of 2026-10-19, condition (b) changed shape. Fires only when the headline XIRR exceeds a 35% sanity ceiling AND the same number, recomputed with the undated opening lineage excluded (alt_rate, already computed either way), is itself back under that same ceiling (or None). The original condition (b) was opening_cost_share ≥ 25% — "is the undated piece a big enough slice of cost" — which turned out to be only a loose proxy for "would removing the undated piece actually explain the implausible number." A 2026-10-19 scenario grid (§4c) found real cases where the proxy and the real answer disagree: a household with a 10–24% opening-lineage share can still show a wildly wrong blended XIRR, silently, under the old design. Comparing rate against alt_rate directly sidesteps the proxy entirely. An XIRR-only trigger (condition (a) alone) would still fire on genuinely excellent but real returns with no evidence of opening-balance dating as the cause — condition (b) still separates "this number is big because of a dating artifact" from "this number is big because the fund genuinely did well," just measured directly now instead of by proxy.
2Why 35%, not some other ceiling — and why there's now only one constant, not two. _XIRR_SANITY_CEILING = 0.35 is a deliberate judgement call, not a derived constant — calibrated against the full synthetic pass/review scenario grid (21 scenarios), the real Chandra Tripathi file, and a further 27-scenario empirical grid run 2026-10-19 specifically to pressure-test the trigger shape itself (§4c) — 0 false positives, 0 false negatives across all three passes combined. _OPENING_SHARE_TRIGGER (the old 25% floor) is removed by the redesign; the same 35% ceiling is now reused for both conditions (the headline rate and the recomputed alt_rate), rather than maintaining two independently-calibrated numbers that turned out to be the same underlying question asked twice. Marked in code with a ponytail-style comment naming _XIRR_SANITY_CEILING as a calibrated judgement call, not a formula.
3Same-scheme-only lineage scoping — no cross-switch tracing. See §3. No persisted switch-pairing exists to trace, and a switch's own date is real, not fabricated, so this is also the philosophically correct stopping point.
4FIFO lot engine reuse, not a new one. _opening_lineage() replays every folio's transactions through the same apply_lot_rules()/cost_per_unit() pair already powering holdings, opening-cost pricing, and conversion-cost — mirroring the existing price_opening_lot() pattern. A redemption consumes the opening lot first under FIFO, exactly as everywhere else in the codebase.
5Proportional terminal-value exclusion, not a second NAV-history fetch. opening_value_remaining = (opening_units_remaining / units_held) × scheme_value, reusing the dashboard's already-computed HoldingRow fields. Exact, not an approximation — one NAV per scheme per day means uniform appreciation across all units of that scheme is a fact, not an assumption.
6Additive-only API — zero change to existing fields. lifetime_xirr/current_holdings_xirr run through the unchanged portfolio_xirr() path; confirmed unchanged for every account across 665 passing tests with zero assertion changes. New fields default to None on both response models.
7alt_xirr can itself be None. When 100% of a metric's cost basis is opening-lineage, excluding it leaves no flows to compute a rate from — None is correct, not a crash. Proven, not assumed.
8current_holdings_xirr's disclaimer is scoped to currently-held schemes only, mirroring how that metric is already scoped — the two disclaimers can legitimately disagree (a distorted fund since fully redeemed disclaims lifetime but not current-holdings).
9"Will this genuinely catch the real distortion, with certainty?" — yes, proven against the real file, not just in theory. See §8: both household members' disclaimers fire with sane, materially different alt_xirr values. Opening-lineage cost shares are 97.85% (Chandra) and 90.28% (Surendra) — both comfortably clear the old 25% floor too, so the real-file result is identical under the old and redesigned trigger; the redesign only changes behavior in the lower-share band this file doesn't sit in.
10Frontend is specified, not built, this round. types.ts, api.ts, the XIRR tile, and a new XirrDisclaimerModal.tsx are the natural surface — matching Root Cause A's own backend-only scope. Tracked as the next step after sign-off, not deferred indefinitely. Corrected: not modeled on OpeningBalanceModal.tsx — see §8b

4b · Four follow-up questions from sign-off review, resolved

aSuperseded 2026-10-19: "25%/35% locked in, unchanged" was this doc's own answer on sign-off review, before the user asked for the trigger to actually be empirically tested rather than re-confirmed. A 15–20-scenario grid (§4c) found a real, reproducible blind spot in the 25% share floor — not in the direction originally suspected (deep-but-finite statement windows going back to 2006/2016), but in a different one: a low opening-lineage share combined with large internal distortion can still swing the blended number past the ceiling, silently. The share floor is removed; _XIRR_SANITY_CEILING = 0.35 is kept, and now gates both the headline rate and the recomputed alt_rate. This correction is made because the user asked for the number to actually be tested rather than re-asserted — not a reversal taken lightly, and not one this doc treats as closed without showing the work; see §4c.
b"Opening balance as purchase price" is already the design — only the date was ever in question. Every opening-balance transaction's cost has always been trusted (it feeds average_nav, amount_invested, every gain figure, and now _opening_lineage()'s cost tracking). Root Cause B only disputes the date, because only XIRR is time-weighted. A more literal reading of the idea — a dateless return for the undated money, XIRR reserved for dated money only — is exactly what alt_xirr already is, read from the other direction. Blending the two into one headline number was considered and rejected: it would silently change lifetime_xirr/current_holdings_xirr for nearly every household, a far bigger, non-additive change, to solve a problem alt_xirr already solves on its own.
cXIRR ≠ CAGR — the "35% CAGR ceiling" wording was imprecise, now fixed throughout. CAGR assumes one lump sum over a fixed period; XIRR solves for the annualized rate across irregular, differently-dated cash flows — they agree only in the single-inflow/single-outflow case. Nothing here computes a CAGR; the 35% figure was never a computed CAGR compared to XIRR, it's a flat sanity benchmark on an annualized rate, informed by what realistic long-run CAGR figures (Nifty 50, top actively-managed funds) look like.

Alternatives considered and deferred, in full:

  • Category-aware ceiling. Liquid/ultra-short debt schemes realistically return ~4–8%/yr (even 20% is generous), while small-cap/sectoral can legitimately touch 40–50% in one exceptional year. A flat 35% ceiling accepts a real blind spot: a distorted debt-fund XIRR at, say, 22% slips through today, since 22% < 35%. Needs a per-sebi_category ceiling table (e.g. 15% debt/liquid, 35% equity, 50% small-cap/sectoral), a cost-weighted blend for the multi-category lifetime_xirr, and a full re-run of the 21-scenario calibration.
  • Benchmark-relative ceiling. Most rigorous on paper: run the same cash-flow dates/amounts against a comparable index's own NAV history (Nifty 50 TRI, Nifty Smallcap 250 TRI, a CRISIL debt index) and flag only if the household's XIRR is some multiple (2–3x) above what the index achieved over that exact period. Directly answers "implausible relative to what was actually achievable," not just a flat number — but needs a benchmark NAV/index time series not fetched anywhere in this path today, plus a second XIRR computation per request.
  • Population-relative / statistical ceiling. Flag outliers vs. the distribution of XIRR across all households. Rejected on two grounds: cold-start (a young product's population isn't stable yet, and synthetic/staging accounts would skew it), and privacy posture (a threshold derived from comparing one household against every other household's finances is worth avoiding by default in a financial app).
Recommendation: keep the flat 35% ceiling — already calibrated against real data, no new data fetch. If a cheap half-step is wanted later: a 2-tier ceiling (lower for debt/liquid, 35% for everything else) closes the debt-fund blind spot above without the full category table or a benchmark feed.
dSuperseded by explicit user override: ship both backend and frontend popup to everyone immediately, no staged rollout. The backend change itself always carried no disruption risk: it's strictly additive — lifetime_xirr/current_holdings_xirr are bit-for-bit unchanged for every account, the two new fields default to None, and an old frontend build simply ignores them. This doc originally recommended walking the frontend popup in gradually (staging/internal first, full rollout after), reasoning that for households like Chandra Tripathi's, the disclaimer's honest message is close to "the large number you've been looking at, and may have already quoted to a spouse or financial advisor, is probably not real" — a legitimate UX shock if badly handled. The user overrode that recommendation explicitly: ship the popup to everyone immediately. Stated reason: Unifolio is currently in internal testing only, with no official/external users looking at the product yet, and the database gets wiped and reseeded routinely between test rounds — so there is no real household that could be shocked by this today, and the staged-rollout concern (support tickets, user trust, confused spouses) doesn't apply to an audience that doesn't exist yet. This is a product-stage decision, not a reversal of the underlying risk assessment — the risk a staged rollout was managing (user confusion/trust, not data safety) is real and should be revisited once Unifolio has actual users.
eWill MF Central / Account Aggregator imports have this problem too? — investigated via code, not assumed. Traced, not guessed: the opening-balance row's date is statement_from — Import.statement_from_date, parsed straight from casparser's own statement_period.from_ field, i.e. the uploaded document's own declared coverage window, not a property of "CAS" as a format. _apply_opening_rule() only writes an opening row when a folio has units predating that window — a since-inception CAS would produce none at all for any folio it fully covers. MF Central/AA (both out of scope today — PRD-01-CAS-Parser-v2.md lines 68/118, AA parked in "a separate future phase") are expected to trigger this far less often, because an API pull is naturally built to request complete history rather than depend on whatever period a PDF covers. Not a verified fact about MF Central's real API — genuinely pre-computerisation holdings or an RTA backfill limit could still leave the same placeholder through any channel. The shipped disclaimer copy should say so honestly, not promise it's eliminated — see §8b.

4c · Empirical scenario-grid testing (2026-10-19, requested explicitly, not offered)

27 scenarios, run against real production code

After item e above, the user raised a specific, concrete concern about the trigger itself: a worry that the 25%/35% design might misfire on CAS statements whose declared coverage window goes back moderately far (to 2006 or 2016) but not all the way, since "there are going to be a lot of files till 2006 that can have opening state but still have opening balances but still have correct values because of our NAV history." And then, explicitly: "Let's make up 10 to 15 scenarios and test this properly … simulate 15 to 20 such scenarios to get a correct, accurate … answer to our plan and update our plans according to our findings."

Method: a scratch script imported the actual production functions — portfolio_xirr() and xirr_string() from app.services.dashboard.xirr, run directly from backend/.venv — and constructed real Transaction-like cash flows for each scenario, run through the real XIRR solver, not a hand-derived approximation. 27 scenarios in total: 19 varying the statement window depth (1–20 years), the true-holding-age ratio (1.0–8.0x), and the growth rate (7% debt through 45% an exceptional equity year) at 100% opening-lineage share; 4 more varying the opening-lineage cost share itself (10–30%) holding the underlying distortion constant; 4 follow-up probes characterizing how severe the low-share gap gets.

Finding 1 — the user's named concern does NOT misfire, empirically confirmed, not merely argued. The distortion depends on the ratio of true holding age to statement window depth, not on how far back the window itself goes. A 10-year window with a 12-year true age (ratio 1.2x, a "statement from ~2016, purchase 2 years earlier" shape) and a 20-year window with a 24-year true age (same 1.2x ratio, a "statement from ~2006" shape) both compute to 18.25% / 14.56% — nowhere near the 35% ceiling, correctly quiet either way. A near-identical ratio at a completely different window depth (15 years) produces the identical 18.25% — direct proof it's the ratio, not the absolute depth, that drives the distortion. Even a decades-old-legacy stress case (20-year window, 40-year true age, a 2.0x ratio) stays just under the ceiling at 32.22%. The user's hypothesis, as stated, turned out not to be the actual gap.
Finding 2 — a different, real gap: the 25% opening-cost-share floor. The share of cost sitting in the undated lineage is not a reliable stand-in for whether that lineage is driving an implausible number, because opening_value_remaining is split proportionally by units held, not by cost (decision-log item 5) — a cheaply-bought opening lot can carry a small share of total cost while still carrying most of a scheme's current value, since it's had far longer to appreciate.

The fix: drop the share floor, compare alt_xirr to the same ceiling directly. alt_xirr was already being computed for every household that passed the old gate — the redesign computes it unconditionally (once rate > 35% and there's a non-zero opening cost to exclude) and fires only if alt_xirr is None or itself ≤ 35%. Verified against all 27 scenarios plus the 4 low-share probes: zero behavior changes anywhere except the four previously-missed low-share rows above — no new false positives introduced on any of the 23 other scenarios, including the window-depth scenarios (Finding 1), a debt-fund case, and a genuinely-high-real-return edge case (ratio 1.0x, g=45% — still fires, since with 100% opening-lineage share there is no alt_xirr to fall back to either way). The two pre-existing tests that exercised the old share floor had fixtures that didn't actually demonstrate this distinction; both were given corrected, realistic fixture numbers, and a new fifth test was added reproducing the exact low-share gap this grid found — see §7.

What this doesn't change: _XIRR_SANITY_CEILING = 0.35 itself, the same-scheme-only lineage scoping, alt_xirr's own computation, or the frontend UX design (§8b) — the popup still shows opening_cost_share, it just no longer gates whether the disclaimer appears at all.

  • What's driving this number — "{opening_cost_share}% of the money behind this return came from an opening balance — units you already held before the period your CAS statement covers.
